# Tone of Voice Guide

## 1. Overall Tone

| Attribute  | Guidance                                                                                |
| ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Register   | Professional but accessible. Write for intelligent colleagues, not academics.           |
| Attitude   | Constructive and solution-oriented. Identify problems plainly, then offer ways forward. |
| Confidence | Assert positions without hedging. Avoid "perhaps", "maybe", "it could be argued".       |
| Warmth     | Neutral to warm. Not cold or distant, but not overly friendly either.                   |

---

## 2. Sentence Structure

### Length

- Favour short sentences. Average 15 words maximum.
- Aim for a 9-year-old reading age. Use the Hemingway Editor to test.
- Use longer sentences sparingly and only when connecting related ideas.
- One idea per sentence.

### Construction

- Lead with the subject. Say what you mean immediately.
- Front-load important information. Don't bury the point.
- Vary sentence openings to avoid monotony, but prioritise clarity over variety.

---

## 3. Paragraph Structure

- Keep paragraphs short: 1–5 sentences maximum.
- One topic per paragraph.
- Use single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis or to state key points.
- White space is your ally. Dense blocks of text signal dense thinking.

---

## 4. Voice

### Active vs Passive

- Default to active voice. Name the actor.
- Use passive voice only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when you want to soften accountability.

**Active:** "The team discovered that the process doesn't work."
**Passive (acceptable):** "Sensitive data is often stored in unsecured locations."

### Person

- Use first person plural ("we", "our") when speaking as the author or organisation, but only when it's clear which organisation "we" refers to. Define "we" early in the document or section.
- Use third person when referring to other teams, users, or external parties.
- Avoid first person singular ("I").

**Do:** "Based on our findings, we recommend action in three areas."
**Don't:** "Based on findings, action is recommended in three areas."

### Contractions

- Use positive contractions sparingly (you'll, we're, it's) to maintain a conversational tone.
- Avoid negative contractions (can't, don't, won't, isn't). Users may misread them as the opposite of what they say.
- Write "cannot" not "can't", "do not" not "don't".
- Avoid complex contractions: "should've", "could've", "would've", "they've".

---

## 5. Tense

Avoid future tense ("will") unless describing certain planned actions. Present tense creates immediacy and confidence.

---

## 6. Word Choice

### Plain English

- Choose common words over formal alternatives.
- If a simpler word exists, use it.

### Words to avoid

Avoid these words. Replace them with specific, concrete alternatives:

| Avoid                             | Use instead                                |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| agenda (unless for a meeting)     | plan                                       |
| advance                           | improve, or be specific                    |
| collaborate                       | work with                                  |
| combat (unless military)          | solve, fix, or be specific                 |
| commit/pledge                     | plan to, we're going to                    |
| deliver (abstract concepts)       | make, create, provide                      |
| deploy (unless military/software) | use, build, create                         |
| dialogue                          | spoke to, discussion                       |
| facilitate                        | be specific about how you're helping       |
| going forward                     | from now on, in the future                 |
| impact (as verb)                  | affect, influence                          |
| initiate                          | start, begin                               |
| key (unless it unlocks something) | important, significant                     |
| leverage                          | use, influence                             |
| overarching                       | usually remove; use encompassing if needed |
| robust                            | comprehensive, well thought out            |
| streamline                        | simplify                                   |
| stakeholders                      | name the actual groups                     |
| utilise                           | use                                        |

### Specificity

- Be concrete. Name tools, teams, and situations.
- Avoid vague references like "various stakeholders" or "certain issues".

### Jargon

- Use technical terms only when necessary and when your audience will understand them.
- Define acronyms on first use, but don't over-explain to a technical audience.
- Avoid management speak: "synergies", "leverage", "holistic approach", "going forward".
- Avoid over-enthusiastic or salesy phrases.

### Latin abbreviations

- Avoid "eg" — screen readers may read it as "egg". Use "for example", "such as", or "like".
- Avoid "ie" — often misunderstood. Use "meaning" or "that is".
- Avoid "etc" — usually unnecessary. If listing examples, "for example" or "such as" signals the list isn't exhaustive.

### Gender-neutral language

- Use "them", "their", "they" for singular individuals where appropriate.
- Prefer "women" and "men" over "females" and "males".

---

## 7. Sensational Tone

Avoid the sensational tone commonly associated with AI-generated content.

Do not fall into any of these typical pitfalls:

- **LinkedIn voice** — the most recognisable LLM tone
- **TED Talk cadence** — short punchy sentences building to false epiphanies
- **Content marketing register** — optimised for engagement, not information
- **Performative directness** — trying hard to sound casual and authoritative simultaneously
- **Synthetic gravitas** — manufactured weight without substance
- **Thought-leader tone** — positioning every observation as insight

### Specific patterns to avoid

| Pattern                                        | Examples                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Faux-casual pivots**                         | "Here's the catch", "Here's the thing", "But here's where it gets interesting", "Let's be honest"   |
| **Pseudo-aphorisms**                           | "You don't X. You Y.", "X isn't about Y. It's about Z.", "The best X doesn't Y — it Z."             |
| **Manufactured certainty**                     | "X is real", "This isn't up for debate", "Full stop.", "Period."                                    |
| **Rhetorical questions with built-in answers** | "Why X? Because Y.", "What does this mean? Everything.", "The answer? It's simpler than you think." |
| **Hyperbolic framing**                         | "The Full Deep Dive", "The Complete Guide", "The Ultimate X", "Everything You Need to Know"         |
| **False intimacy**                             | Constant "you" address, "Let's talk about", "I'm going to let you in on"                            |
| **Cliffhanger structure**                      | "And that changes everything.", "This is where it gets interesting.", "But wait — there's more."    |

---

## 8. Directness

### State problems plainly

- Don't soften failures or gaps. Name them.
- Avoid euphemisms that obscure meaning.

### Make clear recommendations

- Use imperative verbs: "Create", "Establish", "Publish", "Define".
- State what should happen, not what "could be considered".

### Legal and compliance content

- Legal content can still use plain English.
- Use "must" for legal requirements: "You must submit the form by 31 March."
- Use "need to" for administrative requirements: "You need to provide proof of address."

### Acknowledge uncertainty honestly

- When something is unclear, say so directly.
- Don't pretend certainty you don't have.

---

## 9. Emotional Register

### Enthusiasm

- Restrained. Acknowledge positives factually, don't celebrate them.
- Let evidence speak for itself.

### Criticism

- Direct but not harsh. State what's wrong without assigning blame to individuals.
- Focus on systems and outcomes, not people.

### Urgency

- Create urgency through facts and consequences, not through alarming language.
- Avoid "critical", "urgent", "vital", "essential" unless genuinely warranted.

**Do:** "The work stops."
**Don't:** "This creates a critical bottleneck that urgently requires immediate attention."

---

## 10. Document Structure

### Titles

- Keep titles under 65 characters.
- Use sentence case, not title case (except for proper nouns).
- Do not use questions as headings. Users want answers, not questions.
- Front-load with the most important words.

### Summaries

- Keep summaries under 160 characters.
- End with a full stop.
- Front-load with search terms users might use.

### Headers

- Use clear, descriptive headers.
- Keep headers short: 3–6 words.
- Follow proper heading hierarchy: H1, then H2, then H3. Do not skip levels.
- Do not use bold text instead of headings.

### Lists and bullets

- Always use a lead-in line before a list.
- Use numbered lists for sequential items or ranked recommendations.
- Use bullet points for non-sequential items.
- Keep list items parallel in structure.
- Use lower case at the start of each bullet.
- Do not use semicolons at the end of bullets.
- Do not put "or" or "and" after bullets.
- No full stop after the final bullet (unless bullets are complete sentences).
- Avoid over-bulleting. If every paragraph is a bullet, none of them have impact.

### Sections

- Front-load key messages. Put conclusions and recommendations near the top (inverted pyramid).
- Use scenarios or examples to ground abstract points.
- End sections with implications or next steps, not summaries of what was just said.

### Links

- Front-load link text with relevant terms.
- Make links specific and active.
- Never use "click here" or "read more".

**Do:** "Read the project guidelines before starting."
**Don't:** "Click here to read the guidelines."

---

## 11. Numbers, Dates and Formatting

### Numbers

- Use numerals for 2 and above: "2 people", "15 items".
- Write "one" when it appears in running text, unless part of a sequence.
- Use numerals with units: "5kg", "3 hours".

### Dates

- No comma between month and year: "4 June 2017" not "4 June, 2017".
- Use "to" not hyphens in ranges: "Monday to Friday", "9am to 5pm".

### Times

- Use "midnight" not "00:00".
- Use "midday" not "12pm" or "12 noon".
- Format as: "9am", "5:30pm".

### Money

- Use "£75" not "£75.00".
- Write out "pence" in full: "50 pence".

### Ampersands

- Use "and" not "&" in running text.
- Ampersands are acceptable only in logos or official organisation names.

### Capitalisation

- Use sentence case for headings and titles.
- Lower case for "government" unless using the full title.
- Lower case for "minister" unless using the full title with name.
- Never use block capitals for large amounts of text.

---

## 12. Accessibility

All content must meet WCAG 2.2 AA standards.

### Heading hierarchy

- Use one H1 per page (the page title).
- Follow heading levels in order: H1, H2, H3. Never skip levels.
- Do not use bold text as a substitute for headings. Screen readers will not recognise it.

### Link text

- Link text must make sense out of context.
- Avoid "click here", "read more", "this page".
- Describe where the link goes or what it does.

### Images

- All images require alt text describing the content.
- Decorative images should have empty alt text (alt="").

### Screen readers

- Avoid Latin abbreviations (eg, ie) — they may be read incorrectly.
- Avoid ampersands in running text.
- Use proper list markup, not manual bullet characters.

---

## 13. Possession and Attribution

- Use possessive forms naturally: "the company's approach", "the team's decision".
- Attribute findings to their source: "The research showed...", "The guidelines state...".
- Avoid passive constructions that hide who did what.

---

## 14. Qualifying Language

### Avoid over-hedging

- Don't qualify every statement with "may", "might", "could", "potentially".
- State things as they are when you have evidence.

### Use hedges intentionally

- Reserve qualifiers for genuine uncertainty.
- "Often", "usually", "in many cases" are acceptable when patterns aren't universal.

**Do:** "Often, you can't use these tools without their AI constantly scanning the data you upload."

---

## 15. Transitions

- Use simple transitions: "However", "This means", "In practice", "The result".
- Avoid elaborate connectors: "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "In light of the foregoing".
- Let paragraph breaks do transition work where possible.

---

## 16. Rhetorical Devices

### Repetition for emphasis

- Repeat key phrases to reinforce important points.
- Use parallel structure in lists and comparisons.

**Example:** "difficult to find, difficult to apply, and often unclear."

### Concrete scenarios

- Use real examples to illustrate abstract points.
- Describe what actually happens, not what theoretically might happen.

### Questions as structure

- Use questions to frame problems, then answer them — but not in headings.
- Keep questions direct and practical.

**Do:** "Who should they contact? What are the consequences?"
**Don't:** "One might ask what mechanisms exist for escalation and what ramifications might ensue."

---

## 17. Things to Avoid

| Pattern                                       | Why it's problematic                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Starting sentences with "It is" or "There is" | Weak openings that delay the subject                                               |
| Nominalisations (verbs turned into nouns)     | "Make a decision" → "decide"; "carry out an assessment" → "assess"                 |
| Double negatives                              | "Not uncommon" → "common"                                                          |
| Unnecessary adverbs                           | "Very", "really", "extremely", "quite" — delete or find a stronger word            |
| Throat-clearing phrases                       | "It is important to note that", "It should be mentioned that" — just say the thing |
| Scare quotes                                  | Don't put "quotes" or italics around words for "emphasis"                          |
| Exclamation marks                             | Never in formal writing                                                            |
| Negative contractions                         | "Can't", "don't", "won't" — use "cannot", "do not", "will not"                     |
| Latin abbreviations                           | "eg", "ie", "etc" — write them out                                                 |
| Questions as headings                         | Users want answers, not questions                                                  |

---

## 18. Checklist Before Finalising

- [ ] Does every paragraph earn its place?
- [ ] Can any sentence be shortened without losing meaning?
- [ ] Is the average sentence length under 15 words?
- [ ] Are recommendations stated as clear actions?
- [ ] Is there unnecessary repetition?
- [ ] Are all acronyms defined?
- [ ] Does the document front-load its key messages?
- [ ] Would someone outside the team understand this?
- [ ] Does it sound like a person wrote it, not a committee?
- [ ] Are headings in proper hierarchy (no skipped levels)?
- [ ] Is link text descriptive and front-loaded?
- [ ] Are there any negative contractions to remove?
- [ ] Is the title under 65 characters?

---

## Summary

Write like you're explaining something important to a smart colleague. Be direct, be specific, and be human. If a sentence sounds like it came from a generator, rewrite it until it sounds like something you'd actually say.
