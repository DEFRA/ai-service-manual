# Always Validate AI Outputs

AI invented housing statistics for a discovery report. The numbers looked accurate, the formatting was excellent, and the fabricated data nearly made it through.

## Overview

A discovery team was assessing the impact of a new service. The work involved collecting data from multiple sources. They needed to review recent news articles, government policy documents, and historical building records from planning councils. This is typical discovery work.

The team was already using AI extensively. When a new team member joined, they saw AI being used across the project. One team member had used it to pull data from government websites. Another had used it to consolidate information from planning portals. The new person thought this was amazing.

Nobody talked about the crucial differences between what you can trust AI to do and what you cannot. The team showed examples of AI use but did not explain the mindset needed to stay safe.

## What happened

The new team member created a report about new houses built in a specific area of the UK. The report looked fantastic. When they presented it to the team, the team challenged the numbers.

The numbers had been generated by ChatGPT as part of the report. They were completely fabricated. They were grossly inaccurate. Anyone experienced with AI would not be surprised by this. But someone new to AI can easily be fooled when the AI responds with an accurate-looking number.

The sophistication of the report hid the inaccuracy. When a report looks well-designed and professional, people assume the content is accurate too. That was not the case here.

The team caught it because they challenged the numbers. If they had not, the fabricated statistics would have gone into decision-making.

## What this reveals

People who are experienced with AI know that large language models invent facts. They do not retrieve data. They generate text that looks plausible. If you ask for statistics, they will give you statistics. The statistics will look real. They will not be real.

New team members who see AI being used successfully for some tasks will assume it can be used successfully for all tasks. Without guidance on where the boundaries are, they cannot know when they have crossed them.

The presentation matters. A well-formatted, professionally designed output creates trust. If the formatting is good, the content must be good too. This assumption is false but powerful.

## What the team learned

Onboarding needs to highlight risks, not just capabilities. When bringing people onto teams that use AI tools, you cannot just show them what AI can do. You must explain what it cannot do and where the dangers are.

Generic guidance is not enough. Different roles face different risks. Developers face code quality risks. Researchers face data validation risks. The team recognized that role-specific onboarding guides would be more effective than general training.

The guidance should say "go slowly at first" rather than "this will revolutionize your workflow overnight". AI is a powerful and dangerous tool. People need to integrate it gradually into their work, not adopt it in one big hit.

Validation steps must be mandatory for any factual content. If AI produces numbers, statistics, or references, those must be validated before use. This should be part of the process, not an optional extra.

One suggestion was to use AI to review reports. An AI could ask the report writer to prove they have validated all the data in the report. This could work for things like the West Midlands Police incident where a report included invented facts. The AI could challenge the writer to provide sources.

Training alone is not sufficient. Teams need clear processes that require validation. Without mandatory steps, people will skip validation when they are busy or when the output looks convincing.
